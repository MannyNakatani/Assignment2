Assignment 2

Background:

A dataset released on July 25, 2020 that contains more than 12,000 civilian complaints filed against New York City police officers. Contains complaints from September 1985 to January 2020, spanning ~35 years. This dataset does not include allegations that investigators concluded did not occur or were deemed unfounded. In this case, it means if the allegation either was not able to have been confirmed to have happened or confirmed to have violated the NYPD’s rule, then it is not included. In addition, the investigation is done by the Civilian Complaint Review Board, which has limited investigative power.

Source: “ReadMe _ Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers” from CCRB Complaint Data July 2020

Substantiated: The alleged conduct occurred and it violated the rules.
Exonerated: The alleged conduct occurred but did not violate the NYPD’s rules.
Unsubstantiated: The CCRB has fully investigated but could not affirmatively conclude that both the conduct occurred and it broke the rules.

Source: “CCRB Data Layout Table” from CCRB Complaint Data July 2020

White Hat Visualization

White Hat Write-up:

Our white-hat dashboard shows how the allegation records in ProPublica’s NYPD civilian complaints dataset can be broken down by complainant ethnicity and recorded gender. We include allegations from complaints received between 2000 and 2018, regardless of the CCRB finding. Each bar shows a count of allegation records. One complaint can contain multiple allegations, so the bars do not count unique complaints or people.

We use horizontal bars to make the ethnicity labels easier to read and color to distinguish recorded gender. Our first version showed all four allegation types side by side, but smaller categories like "offensive language" were hard to see on the shared scale. The dropdown gives each category the full chart width. We keep the zero baseline and display the axis because the scale can change when viewers switch categories. We also keep records with missing demographic information visible.

The table below the chart answers a different question: what share of all allegations within each ethnicity group are classified as "force"? For example, the dataset contains more Force allegations for black complainants than for Hispanic complainants (3,959 compared with 1,547), but "force" makes up a slightly smaller share of the black group’s allegations (24.3% compared with 25.5%). The table shows both the numerator and denominator so viewers can see how each percentage is calculated. The dropdown changes the bars only.

These comparisons describe a limited release of closed cases involving a selected group of NYPD officers. They cannot establish citywide complaint rates or the likelihood that someone in a demographic group experiences police force.

Black Hat Visualization

Black Hat Write-up:

To try and mislead the viewer about the inequality in force complaints, we have selectively chosen what categories of force complaints to use. In this case, we have chosen to include all three categories for complaints: unsubstantiated, substantiated and exonerated. This is to cherrypick data that supports the idea that police brutality against the black and Hispanic community is decreasing while it is increasing for the Asian and white community. We do this because when we exclude exonerated (no violation of NYPD rules) force complaints, the percentage lines become much ambiguous with no clear trend direction for the black and Hispanic communities.

In addition, we have chosen a specific range of data (2010 - 2018) to cherry pick more of our dataset. This is because when the data is viewed by absolute counts, we see that force complaints from the black and Hispanic community peak around 2010 before starting to fall a little bit. By setting our starting point to be 2010, we create a clear downward trend (even if it's not very steep) that we can exploit.

To increase ambiguity, we name the title of our visualization “Trends in NYPD Violence/Force Complaints by Ethnicity.” By not specifying the date range, we obscure this key fact to try to mislead the viewer subtly. In addition, we named the y-axis “% Change” without specifying that this is “relative percent change since the year 2010”. 2010 is a year we have chosen specifically to create the shown trend lines. In addition, it seems like a standard cutoff for data due to it being a year that ends in 0, which obscures the fact that the year was actually chosen for nefarious reasons.

We have set the y-axis to be the percentile change in force complaints per year since 2010. This is the main way we are trying to deceive our audience. By doing this, it gives the appearance that force complaints from the black and Hispanic communities are decreasing compared to force complaints from the Asian and White communities, which are increasing despite the actual number of reports showing that force complaints from black and Hispanic communities are much higher, no matter the time. In addition, we intentionally leave out the demographic proportions in NYC, so a viewer is not reminded that blacks and Hispanics are minorities but disproportionately suffer the most police force incidents.

Extra Credit Features

Initially, we had four different bar graphs in our white hat visualization to represent the different types of allegations. However, we realized that the smaller categories like "offensive language" were hard to see on the shared scale. To fix this, we added a dropdown to give each category the full chart width. This allows viewers to see the smaller categories more clearly and compare them to the larger categories. This addition also saved us space as we could display all the different types of allegations in a single view.
